Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks Sep 21, 2024

I have been playing with some implementations of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs), the architecture Liu et al. proposed in April 2024. The pitch is simple and a bit provocative: take an MLP, move the nonlinearities from the nodes to the edges, and make them learnable. Every weight becomes a small univariate function, parameterized as a spline. The paper claimed better accuracy per parameter than MLPs, faster neural scaling laws, and networks you can read off as symbolic formulas.

In general, the promising ideas shown in the original paper are not so easy to replicate. I personally believe that KANs are a promising research direction, but the hype around the original paper created an unrealistic expectation of what KANs can do for now.

In this post I want to go through the whole thing properly: the theorem the name comes from, how the architecture relaxes it, how the B-spline edges actually work (with a widget you can drag), a from-scratch KAN in NumPy that runs in your browser against an MLP, and finally where I think KANs stand. For the original exploration, here is the colab where I implemented the models Link to code.

The Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem #

In 1957 Kolmogorov, building on work by his student Arnold on Hilbert’s 13th problem, proved a remarkable statement about continuous functions of several variables.

Theorem (Kolmogorov-Arnold). Let $f : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous. Then there exist continuous univariate functions $\phi_{q,p} : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ and $\Phi_q : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \sum_{q=0}^{2n} \Phi_q\left( \sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p}(x_p) \right).$$

Moreover, the inner functions $\phi_{q,p}$ can be chosen once and for all, independently of $f$. Only the outer functions $\Phi_q$ depend on the target.

Read that again, because it is strange. Every continuous multivariate function is a finite composition of univariate functions and addition. The only truly “multivariate” operation you ever need is $+$. In some sense, there are no genuinely multivariate continuous functions.

So why didn’t this become the foundation of neural networks decades ago? Because the catch is in the regularity. The inner functions guaranteed by the theorem can be extremely rough, fractal-like objects. They are not something you can learn with gradient descent or even write down nicely. Girosi and Poggio made exactly this argument in 1989, in a paper whose title is the whole argument: Kolmogorov’s theorem is irrelevant. For decades that was the consensus.

How KANs relax the theorem #

The theorem has a fixed shape: $n$ inputs, a hidden layer of $2n+1$ sums, one output, and depth two. Liu et al. read this as a two-layer network, $[n, 2n+1, 1]$, where every edge carries a univariate function and every node just sums its inputs. Then they do the deep learning thing: forget about the exact shape and stack such layers, as wide and as deep as you like.

A KAN layer with $n_l$ inputs and $n_{l+1}$ outputs is a matrix of $n_l \times n_{l+1}$ learnable functions $\phi_{j,i}$:

$$x^{(l+1)}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n_l} \phi^{(l)}_{j,i}\left(x^{(l)}_i\right), \qquad \text{KAN}(x) = \left(\Phi^{(L-1)} \circ \cdots \circ \Phi^{(1)} \circ \Phi^{(0)}\right)(x).$$

Compare this with an MLP layer:

$$x^{(l+1)} = \sigma\left(W^{(l)} x^{(l)} + b^{(l)}\right).$$

In an MLP the edges are scalars, linear and learnable, while the nonlinearity $\sigma$ sits on the nodes and is fixed. In a KAN the edges are nonlinear and learnable, and the nodes only add. The bet is that with deep stacks you no longer need the pathological inner functions of the original theorem, and that smooth functions with compositional structure get smooth KAN representations. That bet is a heuristic, not a theorem. The paper does prove an approximation bound, but it assumes the target already has a smooth KAN representation.

A small observation that helped me: since every $\phi$ is a linear combination of fixed basis functions (next section), a KAN layer is a linear layer acting on a fixed per-input feature expansion $x_i \mapsto (B_0(x_i), \dots, B_{G+k-1}(x_i))$. The learnable part is still just a matrix. That is why the implementation below is mostly einsum.

B-spline edges #

Each edge function is parameterized as

$$\phi(x) = w_b \, \text{silu}(x) + \sum_{m=0}^{G+k-1} c_m B_m(x), \qquad \text{silu}(x) = \frac{x}{1 + e^{-x}}.$$

The grid is a set of $G$ equal intervals on the expected input range, say $[-1, 1]$, with knots $t_0 < t_1 < \dots$ spaced $h = 2/G$ apart. The grid is extended by $k$ extra knots on each side, so that every point of $[-1,1]$ is covered by the same number of basis functions.

The basis functions $B_m$ are B-splines of order $k$ (the paper uses $k = 3$, cubic), defined by the Cox-de Boor recursion:

$$B_{m,0}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & t_m \le x < t_{m+1} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

$$B_{m,p}(x) = \frac{x - t_m}{t_{m+p} - t_m} B_{m,p-1}(x) + \frac{t_{m+p+1} - x}{t_{m+p+1} - t_{m+1}} B_{m+1,p-1}(x).$$

Each $B_{m,k}$ is a piecewise polynomial of degree $k$, nonzero only on the $k+1$ intervals $[t_m, t_{m+k+1})$, and on the grid they sum to one. Two properties matter for learning. Local support: changing $c_m$ only moves $\phi$ on those $k+1$ intervals, so different regions of the input are learned by different parameters. Smoothness: cubic splines are $C^2$, so the function and its first two derivatives are continuous and gradients behave.

The residual base term $w_b \, \text{silu}(x)$ is the paper’s version of a skip connection. It gives every edge a reasonable global shape from initialization, and it keeps the edge alive outside the grid, where every B-spline is zero. The original implementation also has a separate scale $w_s$ multiplying the spline. That is redundant with the $c_m$, so I fold it in.

Grid refinement. A trained spline on a grid of $G$ intervals can be transferred to a finer grid of $2G$ intervals by least squares. Because the coarse knots are a subset of the fine ones, the coarse spline space is a subspace of the fine one, and the transfer is exact on the grid. You can train coarse, then refine and keep training with more resolution. The paper calls this grid extension, and it is one of the genuinely nice ideas in it.

Play with a single edge below. Drag the control points (they sit at the Greville abscissae, the average of each basis function’s interior knots, which is where a spline’s control polygon naturally lives), change the order, add the SiLU term, refine the grid, and fit a target.

Counting parameters #

Each edge has $G + k$ spline coefficients plus $w_b$, so a KAN layer has

$$n_l \cdot n_{l+1} \cdot (G + k + 1)$$

parameters, against $n_l \cdot n_{l+1} + n_{l+1}$ for a dense layer. With $G = 5$ and $k = 3$ that is roughly 9 times more parameters per connection. For a stack of $L$ layers of width $N$, a KAN has $O(N^2 L (G + k))$ parameters versus $O(N^2 L)$ for an MLP. The whole argument of the paper is that KANs need a much smaller $N$ to reach the same accuracy, so the comparison only makes sense at matched parameter count, not matched width. That is what I do below.

Concretely, the $[2, 5, 1]$ KAN in the code has $15$ edges. At $G=5$ that is $15 \times 9 = 135$ parameters, and after refining to $G = 10$ it is $15 \times 14 = 210$. The MLP I compare against, $[2, 12, 12, 1]$ with tanh, has $36 + 156 + 13 = 205$.

A tiny KAN from scratch #

Here is a complete KAN in NumPy: the Cox-de Boor basis (and its derivative), a KANLayer with hand-written backprop, grid updates and refinement by least squares, and a plain MLP with the same training loop. The target is the example from the paper,

$$f(x, y) = \exp\left(\sin(\pi x) + y^2\right),$$

which a $[2, 1, 1]$ KAN can represent exactly: inner functions $\sin(\pi x)$ and $y^2$, with $\exp$ as the outer function. Both models train with full-batch Adam on the same 500 points and are evaluated on 2000 fresh ones. A KAN step costs about three times an MLP step, so the MLP gets three times as many steps, which gives both models roughly the same compute budget. The gradients are derived by hand (I checked them against finite differences). Press Run. It takes about six seconds in the browser.

So on this problem the KAN wins by more than an order of magnitude in test MSE at roughly the same parameter count and compute. Changing the seed moves the numbers around (KAN test MSE between about $7 \times 10^{-6}$ and $4 \times 10^{-5}$, MLP between about $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $3 \times 10^{-3}$), but not the ordering. Widening the MLP to 337 parameters and training it for 10,000 steps (on 1000 training points) got it to about $5 \times 10^{-5}$, which narrows the gap but does not close it. I also tried a target without additive structure, $\sin(4xy)/(1+x^2)$, and the KAN still came out ahead.

I want to be honest about what this does and doesn’t show:

  • This is the KAN’s home turf. A smooth, two-dimensional function that is literally a composition of univariate functions and a sum. It is the paper’s own showcase. Low-dimensional smooth regression is where splines have always been excellent, KAN or not.
  • The KAN needed babysitting. With a fixed grid, hidden activations drift outside the second layer’s grid, where the splines vanish. In an early version of this script, some test points landed outside the grid and test MSE jumped to about $10^2$. The periodic grid updates are not optional decoration.
  • The KAN is slower. Each step takes 3 to 4 times as long as an MLP step here, and that is with a vectorized basis on a tiny network. The MLP is a couple of matrix multiplies. At matched width, the KAN’s matrix multiply is also $G + k + 1$ times larger, on top of evaluating the basis.

Edit the target function and try something without additive structure, or add noise to ytr. It is the quickest way to form your own opinion.

Where KANs stand #

Interpretability and symbolic regression. This is the most distinctive part of the paper. After training with a sparsity penalty, you prune unused edges and look at each remaining $\phi$. If it looks like $\sin$ or $x^2$ or $\exp$, you snap it to that symbolic form (fitting an affine reparameterization) and continue training. On toy physics and math problems this recovers exact formulas, which an MLP cannot give you so directly. The follow-up, KAN 2.0, leans fully into this “AI for science” angle. It adds multiplication nodes and tools for putting domain knowledge into KANs and pulling formulas out. But this is interpretability of small networks on low-dimensional, mostly symbolic problems. A KAN with thousands of edges is no easier to read than an MLP.

Fair comparisons. Yu, Yu and Wang (2024) compared KANs and MLPs at matched parameters and FLOPs across machine learning, vision, audio, NLP, and symbolic formula tasks. MLPs generally won everywhere except symbolic formula representation. Their ablations attribute that advantage mainly to the B-spline activation itself, and giving an MLP B-spline activations closed most of that gap. They also found that KANs forget more in class-incremental continual learning, which contradicts one of the original paper’s claims. My toy result above is consistent with this: splines are great at smooth low-dimensional functions.

Training cost and speed. The original paper is upfront that its KANs train about 10 times slower than MLPs with the same number of parameters. Evaluating a spline basis is not a single fused matrix multiply, and grid updates add extra machinery to training. A lot of follow-up work is about speed. FastKAN, for example, replaces the cubic B-splines with Gaussian radial basis functions (they look very similar) and observes that the result is a radial basis function network. That observation is a useful corrective in itself: a lot of the KAN design space is old ideas (splines, basis expansions, RBF networks, additive models) arranged in a new way.

My take. KANs are a genuinely nice idea for small scientific problems where you want accuracy on smooth low-dimensional functions and a formula at the end. As a general replacement for MLPs, the evidence just isn’t there yet, and the original framing oversold it. That’s fine. A good idea with an oversized launch is still a good idea, and I expect the useful parts (learnable activations, grid refinement, spline features) to survive in other architectures.
