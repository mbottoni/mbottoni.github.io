Classifier free diffusion guidance Dec 15, 2024

One of the key techniques in diffusion models that has significantly improved their performance is classifier-free guidance. In this post, we’ll explore what classifier-free guidance is, how it works, and implement it from scratch in PyTorch.

Before any equations, here is the whole idea running in the page. The data is a small mixture of Gaussians with three classes, so the conditional and unconditional scores are known exactly and the only thing being changed is the guidance scale. Drag w and watch what happens to the samples.

What is Classifier-Free Guidance? #

At its core, classifier-free guidance is an elegant technique that allows us to control the generation process of diffusion models without requiring a separate classifier. The key insight is that we can create a more powerful conditional generation process by combining both conditional and unconditional generation in a clever way.

Think of it like having two artists working together:

  1. One artist (conditional model) who follows specific instructions
  2. One artist (unconditional model) who creates freely without constraints

By combining their perspectives with different weights, we can create results that are both high-quality and well-aligned with our desired conditions.

Where the Formula Comes From #

That equation is easy to use but it is not obvious why it works, so here is the derivation. I follow the notation of Ho and Salimans, which is also what the code below uses. A noisy latent is $z_\lambda = \alpha_\lambda x + \sigma_\lambda \epsilon$, where $x$ is a data point, $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, $\lambda$ is the log signal-to-noise ratio $\log(\alpha_\lambda^2/\sigma_\lambda^2)$, and $\alpha_\lambda^2 + \sigma_\lambda^2 = 1$. The class label (or text prompt) is $c$, and $\varnothing$ is the special “no condition” token. All gradients below are with respect to $z_\lambda$.

Noise predictions are scaled scores #

The fact that ties everything together is that a denoiser trained with the $\epsilon$-prediction loss estimates the score of the noisy marginal. By Tweedie’s formula, the optimal predictor is $\epsilon^*(z_\lambda) = \mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid z_\lambda]$, and it satisfies

$$\epsilon^*(z_\lambda) = -\sigma_\lambda\, \nabla_{z_\lambda} \log p(z_\lambda), \qquad \epsilon^*(z_\lambda, c) = -\sigma_\lambda\, \nabla_{z_\lambda} \log p(z_\lambda \mid c).$$

So any statement about scores is also a statement about $\epsilon$-predictions, up to the factor $-\sigma_\lambda$. A linear combination of scores is a linear combination of noise predictions with the same coefficients.

Classifier guidance: Bayes on scores #

Before CFG, Dhariwal and Nichol steered diffusion with a separately trained classifier $p_\phi(c \mid z_\lambda)$ that works on noisy inputs. Bayes’ rule gives $p(z_\lambda \mid c) = p(c \mid z_\lambda)\, p(z_\lambda) / p(c)$, and $p(c)$ does not depend on $z_\lambda$, so it vanishes when we take the gradient:

$$\nabla \log p(z_\lambda \mid c) = \nabla \log p(z_\lambda) + \nabla \log p(c \mid z_\lambda).$$

Classifier guidance multiplies the classifier term by a weight. Applied to a conditional model with weight $w$, as in Ho and Salimans, the guided prediction is

$$\tilde\epsilon(z_\lambda, c) = \epsilon_\theta(z_\lambda, c) - w\, \sigma_\lambda\, \nabla \log p_\phi(c \mid z_\lambda) \approx -\sigma_\lambda\, \nabla\big[\log p(z_\lambda \mid c) + w \log p_\phi(c \mid z_\lambda)\big],$$

which is the scaled score of $\tilde p(z_\lambda \mid c) \propto p(z_\lambda \mid c)\, p_\phi(c \mid z_\lambda)^{w}$.

Removing the classifier #

The trick in CFG is that a conditional and an unconditional model together already define a classifier, through Bayes’ rule in the other direction:

$$p^{i}(c \mid z_\lambda) \propto \frac{p(z_\lambda \mid c)}{p(z_\lambda)},$$

so its gradient needs no extra network:

$$\nabla \log p^{i}(c \mid z_\lambda) = \nabla \log p(z_\lambda \mid c) - \nabla \log p(z_\lambda) = -\frac{1}{\sigma_\lambda}\big[\epsilon^*(z_\lambda, c) - \epsilon^*(z_\lambda)\big].$$

Plug this “implicit classifier” into the classifier-guidance formula in place of $p_\phi$, and use the network’s predictions for the exact ones:

$$\tilde\epsilon(z_\lambda, c) = \epsilon_\theta(z_\lambda, c) + w\big[\epsilon_\theta(z_\lambda, c) - \epsilon_\theta(z_\lambda, \varnothing)\big] = (1+w)\, \epsilon_\theta(z_\lambda, c) - w\, \epsilon_\theta(z_\lambda, \varnothing).$$

That is the formula from the previous section. One network supplies both terms because the condition is replaced by $\varnothing$ with probability $p_{\text{uncond}}$ during training (the puncond argument in the training loop below).

Which distribution it targets #

In score form, the guided prediction corresponds to

$$\nabla \log p(z_\lambda \mid c) + w\, \nabla \log p^{i}(c \mid z_\lambda) = \nabla \log p(z_\lambda) + (1+w)\, \nabla \log p^{i}(c \mid z_\lambda),$$

so at each noise level it is the score of the tilted density

$$\tilde p_w(z_\lambda \mid c) \;\propto\; p(z_\lambda)\, p(c \mid z_\lambda)^{1+w} \;\propto\; p(z_\lambda \mid c)^{1+w}\, p(z_\lambda)^{-w}.$$

A note on conventions, since this trips people up. In this post (and in Ho and Salimans), $w = 0$ means plain conditional sampling with no guidance, and the classifier exponent is $1 + w$. Many codebases, Stable Diffusion among them, write the same thing as $\tilde\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(z, \varnothing) + s\,[\epsilon_\theta(z, c) - \epsilon_\theta(z, \varnothing)]$ with a “guidance scale” $s = 1 + w$. There $s = 1$ means no guidance, and a typical $s = 7.5$ corresponds to $w = 6.5$ here.

There is an important caveat. The tilted density is defined separately at every noise level, and the family $\{\tilde p_w(z_\lambda \mid c)\}_\lambda$ is in general not the set of noised marginals of a single clean distribution $p(x)\, p(c \mid x)^{1+w}$. Also, the difference $\epsilon_\theta(z, c) - \epsilon_\theta(z, \varnothing)$ need not be the gradient of any actual classifier. So “CFG samples from $p(x)\, p(c \mid x)^{1+w}$” is the right intuition but only an approximation. In practice it is a very effective heuristic, not an exact sampler.

Why large $w$ kills diversity #

The exponent $1 + w$ acts as an inverse temperature on the implicit classifier. As $w$ grows, $\tilde p_w$ puts more and more of its mass where $p(c \mid z)$ is largest. It rewards the inputs that are most unambiguously class $c$, not the typical ones. You can see this exactly in a one-dimensional Gaussian toy. Take $p(x \mid c) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_c, s^2)$ and an unconditional $p(x) = \mathcal{N}(0, s_0^2)$ that is broader, $s_0 > s$, as it typically is since it mixes all classes. Then $p(x \mid c)^{1+w} p(x)^{-w}$ is again Gaussian, and collecting the quadratic and linear terms in the exponent gives

$$\tilde s_w^2 = \Big(\frac{1+w}{s^2} - \frac{w}{s_0^2}\Big)^{-1}, \qquad \tilde\mu_w = \tilde s_w^2\, \frac{(1+w)\,\mu_c}{s^2}.$$

The variance $\tilde s_w^2$ decreases monotonically in $w$ and goes to zero like $\frac{s^2}{w\,(1 - s^2/s_0^2)}$, so samples collapse onto a point. The mean does not stay at $\mu_c$. It is pushed away from the unconditional mean towards $\mu_c\, s_0^2/(s_0^2 - s^2)$, past the class centre. These two effects are the low-diversity and oversaturated “too much of the class” look that large guidance weights produce on images. That is the fidelity versus diversity trade-off (FID against Inception Score) that Ho and Salimans sweep over $w$.
